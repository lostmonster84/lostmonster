## What This Covers

Homepage copy -- headlines, subheadlines, CTA text, social proof framing, section headings, and the narrative arc that carries visitors from hero to conversion.

**This supplement is universal.** It must NEVER reference a specific project, brand, colour, or client. It teaches the craft -- the patterns that make this job type work regardless of who it's for. The project's design guide handles brand identity. This handles quality.

## Owner's Validated Patterns

> Cross-referenced from 6 shipped projects. Full research: `researchers/supplements/SCOUTX-owner-project-patterns.md`

### Copy Constants (Validated Across 6 Projects)

| # | Pattern | How to Apply |
|---|---------|-------------|
| 1 | **Two voice modes** | Hospitality: warm, inviting, personal ("The Scotland of Your Imagination", "Cosy Highland Lodges"). SaaS: direct, confident, conversational ("Stop sharing boring links. Share a Slyde.", "Win more work with better tender responses."). Identify the mode before writing |
| 2 | **Action-specific CTAs, never generic** | "Check Availability" (Ancarraig), "Create your first Slyde" (Slydes), "Get early access" (Evidis), "Search to rent" (DOMA). The CTA names the exact outcome. Never "Learn more" or "Get started" alone |
| 3 | **Friction reducers always present** | "No credit card. Live in minutes." (Slydes), "Takes 2 minutes" (HospoJobs), "Welcoming guests since 1973" (Ancarraig). One line below every CTA. Under 8 words |
| 4 | **Eyebrow labels set context** | "Guest Stories" above testimonials (Ancarraig), "Security & Privacy" above vault section (Evidis), "For Agents" (DOMA). Tiny, uppercase, sets expectation for the section below |
| 5 | **Conversational closer** | "Running a restaurant, bar, hotel, or venue? We should talk." (Slydes). The final section before CTA speaks directly, breaks formality. Feels human |
| 6 | **Specifics over superlatives** | "12 Scandinavian-style lodges on 12 acres" (Ancarraig), "Reservations went up 35%" (Slydes), "AES-256 encrypted, UK hosted" (Evidis). Numbers and specifics, never "the best" or "world-class" |
| 7 | **No em dashes in shipped copy** | Owner rule. Use commas, full stops, or restructure. Em dashes are flagged as AI-flavoured punctuation |

## Patterns (What the Best All Do)

### Pattern 1: Hero Headline = Outcome, Not Mechanism
**What:** The headline names what the user gets or feels, not how the product works. The subheadline explains the mechanism. This division is consistent across all top-performing homepages.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Railway (#4: "Ship software peacefully" = feeling), Notion (#3: "Zero busywork" = outcome), Superhuman (#11: "Superpowers" = aspiration), Pitch (#6: "Don't just present. Pitch." = upgrade). Contrast: Retool's "Generate internal software better with AI" explains mechanism in the headline -- weaker.
**How to apply:** Write the headline last. First write what the product does (that's the subheadline). Then distill the benefit into 3-8 words. Test: does the headline work without the subheadline? If it needs explanation, it's a subheadline, not a headline.

### Pattern 2: 3-8 Word Headline Length
**What:** Top-performing hero headlines are punchy. The sweet spot is 3-8 words. Longer headlines dilute impact and compete with the visual for attention.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Railway (#4: 3 words), Notion (#3: 4 words), Raycast (#5: 4 words), Pitch (#6: 4 words), Loom (#7: 8 words). No studied homepage uses a headline over 10 words.
**How to apply:** Draft long, then cut. If your headline is over 8 words, move the excess to the subheadline. Every word must earn its place. Articles ("the", "a") are usually cuttable. Subheadline: 15-30 words.

### Pattern 3: CTA Copy = Action Verb + Benefit
**What:** CTA buttons use an action verb paired with a benefit signal. Not just "Submit" or "Click here" -- the button text tells you what happens AND what you get.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Vercel (#1: "Start Deploying"), Notion (#3: "Get Notion free"), Clerk (#13: "Start building for free"), Framer (#8: "Start for free"), Retool (#9: "Start for free" + "Book a demo")
**How to apply:** Primary CTA formula: [Action verb] + [benefit/free signal]. "Start for free", "Get started", "Deploy now", "Try it free". Secondary CTA formula: [Lower-commitment action] + [context]. "Get a demo", "Contact sales", "Watch video". Never: "Submit", "Click here", "Learn more" (without context).

### Pattern 4: Section Headlines Tell the Full Story
**What:** A visitor who reads only the section headlines (skipping all body copy) should understand the full value proposition. Headlines form a narrative arc.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Stripe (#2: sections progress from "What it does" to "Who uses it" to "Why trust it" to "Get started"). Clerk (#13: consistent "Explore X" headings create scannable sections).
**How to apply:** Write all section headlines first, in order. Read them as a sequence. Do they tell a story? The arc should be: [What you get] > [Why it works] > [Who uses it] > [Proof it works] > [Get started]. If a headline could belong to any product, it's too generic.

### Pattern 5: Quantified Testimonials (Numbers, Not Praise)
**What:** The most persuasive testimonials contain specific numbers, named competitors, or measurable outcomes. "Great product" is worthless. "$6M saved" is persuasive.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Retool (#9: "$6M saved" -- DoorDash), Stripe (#2: "$1.9T processed"), PlanetScale (#12: "45ms to 5ms"), Cal.com (#10: "More elegant than Calendly"), Vercel (#1: "7m to 40s build times")
**How to apply:** Every testimonial should contain at least one of: a specific metric, a named competitor replaced, a time/money outcome, or a before/after comparison. If a testimonial is just praise without specifics, either get a better quote or add context brackets: "...reduced our deploy time from 45 minutes to 90 seconds."

### Pattern 6: Consistent CTA Verb Across the Page
**What:** The primary CTA uses the same verb throughout the page. Secondary variations are acceptable but the core action should be recognizable at every placement.
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Clerk (#13: "Explore X" everywhere), Loom (#7: 6x "Get Loom for free"), Cal.com (#10: "Get started" repeated), Retool (#9: "Start for free" in nav, hero, mid-page, footer)
**How to apply:** Pick one primary CTA text. Place it in hero, mid-page, and closing section. Variations are fine for secondary CTAs ("Book a demo" / "Talk to sales") but the primary action verb should be identical. This creates pattern recognition -- the user learns what the "next step" looks like.

### Pattern 7: Friction Reducer Text Below CTA
**What:** Small text below or beside the CTA button that actively reduces perceived risk: "No credit card required", "Free forever for small teams", "Cancel anytime".
**Applicability:** Universal
**Evidence:** Cal.com (#10: SSO as friction reducer), Clerk (#13: "Start building for free" implies no cost), Neon (free tier messaging), Better Stack (explicit "Get started in 30 seconds")
**How to apply:** Add one friction-reducing line within 16px of every CTA button. Under 8 words. Address the primary objection: signup = "No credit card required", free trial = "Cancel anytime", download = "Free, no account needed". This is not optional -- it measurably improves conversion.

### Pattern 8: Competitor Displacement Through Copy (Not Tables)
**What:** Acknowledge competitors indirectly through testimonials, calculators, or "replaces X" copy rather than comparison tables. The homepage should position, not compare.
**Applicability:** Products with clear competitors
**Evidence:** Railway (#4: each feature lists alternatives it replaces), Notion (#3: cost calculator vs 10 tools), Cal.com (#10: testimonials mention Calendly switch), PlanetScale (#12: latency graphs without naming comparison tables)
**How to apply:** Let social proof do the competitive work. Testimonials that mention switching ("We moved from X to Y and...") are more credible than comparison tables. If you must compare, use a calculator or before/after section, not a feature grid.

## Anti-Patterns (What to Avoid)

| # | Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails | Example |
|---|-------------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | Category-description headlines ("The modern X platform") | Tells the visitor the category, not the benefit. Wastes the 3-5 second decision window | "The AI-powered project management tool" -- describes category, not outcome |
| 2 | "Learn more" without context | Screen readers announce it identically for every instance. Users can't distinguish what they'll learn. Zero information scent | 5 different "Learn more" links on one page, each going to different destinations |
| 3 | Passive voice in CTAs | "Your demo can be booked" vs "Book a demo." Active voice drives action. Passive creates distance | "Get started" is active (good). "A free trial can be started" is passive (bad) |
| 4 | Wall-of-text value propositions | Users scan, not read. Paragraphs in feature sections get skipped entirely. The copy should be as visual as the design | Feature sections with 3+ paragraphs of body text that no one reads |
| 5 | Generic social proof ("Thousands of happy customers") | No specificity = no credibility. A named person with a title and a number is worth 100 anonymous "customers" | Star ratings without context, unnamed testimonials, round numbers ("10,000+ users") |

## Benchmarks

| Element | Target | Source |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Hero headline | 3-8 words | Railway 3, Notion 4, Raycast 4, Loom 8 |
| Hero subheadline | 15-30 words | Enough to explain; short enough to scan |
| CTA text | 2-4 words | "Get started", "Start for free", "Deploy now" |
| Friction reducer | Under 8 words | "No credit card required", "Free forever" |
| Section headlines | 3-10 words, benefit-focused | Scannable narrative arc |
| Body copy per section | 30-80 words maximum | Users scan; more gets skipped |
| Total homepage copy | 1,000-2,000 words (standard SaaS) | Minimal: 500-800, Complex: 2,500-4,000 |
| Testimonial length | 20-50 words with at least 1 specific metric | Short enough to read, specific enough to persuade |
| Logo bar label | 3-8 words | "Trusted by 400,000+ companies" |

### Copy Quality Checklist

| Test | How to check |
|------|-------------|
| Headline works without subheadline | Cover the subheadline -- does the headline still communicate value? |
| CTA is specific | Would a visitor know exactly what happens after clicking? |
| No jargon | Would a non-technical decision-maker understand every word? |
| No superlatives without proof | Is every "best", "fastest", "most" backed by a number? |
| Testimonials have specifics | Does every quote contain a metric, a competitor name, or a measurable outcome? |

## Mobile Patterns

| Copy Element | Desktop | Mobile (390px) |
|-------------|---------|----------------|
| Hero headline | 48-72px, centred or left | 28-36px, centred, full-width |
| Hero subheadline | 15-30 words | May need trimming to 10-20 words |
| CTA text | Same on both | Same (never abbreviate CTAs for mobile) |
| Section headlines | 48-56px | 24-32px |
| Body copy | 2-3 short paragraphs | Same or slightly trimmed |
| Testimonial display | 2-3 visible | 1 visible, swipeable |

**Key rule:** Never truncate or hide CTA text on mobile. The conversion action must be identical on both viewports.

## Accessibility Patterns

### Copy a11y
- CTA button text must be descriptive in isolation (screen readers announce buttons out of context)
- Multiple "Learn more" links must use `aria-label="Learn more about [specific topic]"` to differentiate
- Testimonial quotes should use `<blockquote>` with `cite` attribute
- Don't rely on visual formatting (bold, colour) alone to convey meaning -- add structural markup

### Heading Structure
- Hero headline = `<h1>` (single instance)
- Section headlines = `<h2>` (sequential, no skipping levels)
- Subsection headlines = `<h3>`
- Screen reader users navigate by heading level -- broken hierarchy = broken navigation

---

## Performance Patterns

Copy-relevant performance:
- Hero text must render before hero visual (text is smaller, faster, gives immediate context)
- System fonts or preloaded web fonts for hero headline -- no FOIT (flash of invisible text)
- CTA buttons in hero should be interactive within 2s (no JS-dependent rendering)

## Planning Implications

See CODAX supplement for full planning guidance. Key points for WORDX:
- Copy should be written BEFORE design/build. The story drives the layout, not the reverse
- Social proof data (logos, testimonials, metrics) is a dependency -- start collecting in week 1
- Homepage copy has the most stakeholder opinions. Expect 2-3 review rounds minimum
- Write all section headlines as a narrative arc first, then fill in body copy per section
